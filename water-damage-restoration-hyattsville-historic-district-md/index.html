Water Damage Restoration in the Hyattsville Historic District

Water damage restoration in the Hyattsville Historic District starts with the house in front of you: mostly frame construction, older masonry foundations and tight lots. The 24/7 line sends licensed and insured professionals who dry it with the building in mind.

A district of frame houses, brick foundations and back alleys

Water damage restoration in the Hyattsville Historic District has to account for how these houses were built. The National Register nomination, a federal listing document, counts roughly 600 structures, mostly houses, and says over 90 percent contribute to the district. It lists Queen Anne, Carpenter Gothic, Italianate and Second Empire houses, along with cottages, duplexes and bungalows.

The same nomination says most residences are frame, with clapboard, German siding and shingles common. Older houses sit on brick foundations, a few on fieldstone, and newer ones on formstone or concrete. That matters for a restoration crew because wood siding, trim and sills dry differently from masonry, so a moisture meter has to be read material by material.

The nomination also describes a patchwork of narrow streets and back alleys, with mature trees and consistent front setbacks. Tight access changes the practical side of a job: where hoses and extraction units can be staged, where drying equipment can plug in, and how a crew reaches a rear entrance. Tell the dispatcher if your house is reached by an alley.

Three street names that appear in both the nomination and city flood notices

Gallatin Street, Hamilton Street and 40th Place all appear in the National Register nomination. They also appear in city records about heavy rain. A city flash flood alert issued on 10 August 2022 reported roads closed at the intersections of 40th Place and Hamilton Street and of 40th Place and Gallatin Street. A local news column on the city's Lower Ward 1 stormwater study names Hamilton Street and Gallatin Street among its trouble spots.

Read that carefully. The reports name specific intersections, not the whole district, and this page does not claim that any house, any block or the district as a whole is affected. We found no source that confirms flooding inside the historic district itself. We also found nothing in the nomination about creeks, drainage or basements, so we do not guess about them.

The 10 September 2020 storm was different in scale. A local newspaper reported more than six inches of rain in Hyattsville and homes damaged across the city, but it did not name streets. Treat that as a city-wide event, not as damage confirmed in this neighborhood. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified. To check a single address, search it in the FEMA Flood Map Service Center at msc.fema.gov.

What to check before you call the line

A few minutes of looking gives the dispatcher what is needed to send the right people and equipment. None of this should be done standing in water.

Quick checks from a dry spot

  • ✓Find the source if it is inside the house: a supply line, a water heater, an appliance hose or a radiator or heating pipe. Close the nearest shutoff valve if you can reach it safely.
  • ✓Note what is wet: flooring, baseboards, the lower part of walls, ceilings below a bathroom. Water in older frame walls can travel behind trim, so look beyond the visible stain.
  • ✓Decide where the water came from. Clean supply water is treated differently from water that came up through a floor drain or toilet, which should be handled as sewage until a professional says otherwise.
  • ✓Take photos and short videos of every wet area and of any damaged belongings before you move them.
  • ✓Tell the dispatcher how the house is reached, such as a front street or a back alley, and whether the area where the water sits is a finished space, a utility room or a crawl space.

Electrical and sewage safety before anything else

Stay out of the water if power may be on

Do not walk into standing water if outlets, cords, appliances or the electrical panel might be live. Switch off breakers for wet rooms only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water. If you cannot, stay back and call your electric utility or 911.

  • Shut off the main water valve only if it is safe and reachable.
  • If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 and the gas company from outside.
  • Do not run household fans or a vacuum on sewage or other contaminated water. They can spread contamination into the air and the rest of the house.
  • For a sewer backup, WSSC Water keeps a 24-hour emergency line at 301-206-4002. WSSC says the owner maintains the sewer line from the property line to the building.
  • Call 911 for immediate danger to people. Then call (301) 936-0619 for dispatch.

Drying older siding, trim and finishes without tearing out more than needed

The aim in any older house is to dry what can be dried and remove only what cannot be saved. No source we found says what is inside the walls of houses in this district, so we do not assume plaster, lath or any particular wall material. Older houses in general can have a mix of original and later materials, and a technician should identify them on site.

Materials that stay wet are the ones that cause trouble. Contaminated carpet pad and soaked insulation usually have to go. Solid wood trim, flooring and framing can often be dried in place when the water was clean and drying starts quickly. Brick and other masonry hold moisture and release it slowly, so readings are taken over several days, not once.

Matching old finishes is a rebuild question, not a drying question. A crew can tell you what has to be opened up and what can stay, and the water damage repair stage afterward deals with new drywall, trim and paint. Ask for the scope in writing before work starts. If the house has historic features you want to protect, say so on the first call.

How extraction and drying work in a wet older house

  1. 1

    Extract standing water

    Extraction units pull standing water from floors first. This is the fastest way to stop water wicking into framing, so it comes before drying equipment. For active flooding, see emergency water removal.

  2. 2

    Map the moisture

    Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera show where water traveled, including behind baseboards and under flooring. Readings are recorded so the crew can show what changed from day to day.

  3. 3

    Dry with air and dehumidification

    Air movers push dry air across surfaces while LGR dehumidifiers pull moisture out of the air. This is the core of structural drying, and ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard for professional water damage restoration.

  4. 4

    Verify before closing up

    Walls, floors and cavities are re-read with the meters. Materials are closed up only when readings show they are dry, and a mold check follows if anything stayed wet.

Paperwork that helps an insurance claim

Start with photos and video of the damage before cleanup, then keep the moisture readings and an itemized scope of work from the restoration company. Save receipts for emergency steps you take yourself. Check your policy, because coverage depends on the cause of the loss and on the policy terms, and the company cannot promise payment or approval.

The Maryland Insurance Administration says most homeowners policies do not cover flooding, which usually comes from a separate flood policy. It says sewer or drain backup typically needs an endorsement, and that Maryland law requires insurers to offer it. It also says foundation seepage is generally not covered. Ask your insurer which category your loss falls into before you assume anything.

Mistakes that make a wet older house worse

Avoid these in the first two days

Waiting to see if it dries on its own is the most common one. The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas and items within 24 to 48 hours to help prevent mold growth, and mold needs moisture to grow.

  • Painting or covering wet siding, trim or walls before readings show they are dry.
  • Pulling out wall materials before anyone has measured moisture, which can remove more than needed.
  • Treating drain or toilet backup water as clean.
  • Assuming the stain is the whole problem. Water often travels past what you can see, so a mold remediation question may come up later if drying is delayed.
