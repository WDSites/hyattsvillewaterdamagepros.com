How to Remove Mold From Basement Walls in Hyattsville, MD

Hard basement walls can often be cleaned. Soft ones usually cannot. Either way, the mold returns if the moisture does.

Hard walls can be cleaned, soft walls usually cannot

To remove mold from a basement wall, first identify the material. The EPA says to scrub mold off hard surfaces with detergent and water and to dry completely. That method suits painted block, poured concrete and sealed masonry. Porous drywall, paneling and insulation that have grown mold are normally cut out and replaced.

The EPA also gives a size line: if the moldy area is less than about 10 square feet, many homeowners can handle it. Past that, or if the water was dirty, a professional is the safer call.

Detergent and water, done safely

  1. 1

    Protect yourself

    Wear gloves, eye protection and a well-fitted respirator. Open a window or run a fan that exhausts outdoors, and keep the dust down.

  2. 2

    Mist and scrub

    Dampen the area lightly so spores do not rise, then scrub with a stiff brush and detergent in water. Work in small sections.

  3. 3

    Wipe and dry

    Wipe the wall with clean water and dry it completely with towels, fans and a dehumidifier. A moisture meter confirms it is dry.

  4. 4

    Bag the waste

    Seal brushes, rags and any removed material in plastic bags before carrying them through the house.

Bleach: what EPA and CDC actually say

Bleach is not the default answer

The EPA says bleach is not recommended as a routine practice during mold cleanup. On hard surfaces, detergent and water does the cleaning, and drying does the rest.

The CDC adds that you should never use bleach in a closed space, and never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleansers. A basement with little airflow is exactly that kind of space.

  • Do not combine bleach with any other cleaning product.
  • Ventilate the room before and during any cleaning.
  • If floodwater or sewage touched the wall, stop and call a professional.

Find the water before you scrub

A basement wall touches cool soil on one side and indoor air on the other. Cool surfaces collect condensation, and masonry can wick groundwater through its pores. Neither needs a flood to keep the wall damp enough for growth.

Stored boxes pushed against the wall block airflow and make it worse. Pulling items a few inches away, running a dehumidifier and watching for new stains are easy steps to try before any cleanup.

Mold needs moisture, so cleaning without fixing the cause is a short-term answer. Check the gutter downspouts first. Water that drops next to the foundation wall can soak through block, so extend the discharge away from the house.

Then check the sump pump if you have one. Test it by pouring water into the pit and watching it run. The city's brochure says sump pump water must not be directed onto a neighboring property or over the public right of way, so route the discharge with that in mind.

Look at the inside of the wall too. A stain line that sits low and runs along the floor suggests groundwater or a leak at the wall-floor joint. Stains higher up, or near a window, suggest water entering from outside or from a pipe above. Marking the line with a pencil and the date shows whether it grows after rain.

Hyattsville, MD: what the city's stormwater study says

The city's own Lower Ward 1 Resilient Stormwater Systems Study says 41st Street experiences standing water during and after intense rain. It notes that frequent downpours in 2018, the wettest year on record for the Washington, DC area, spurred multiple flood events.

The study also says much of the storm drain system dates from 1960 to 1990, and that a Hyattsville-Riverdale levee system protects some areas from the 100-year flood. For a basement, the lesson is practical: a low street or a clogged inlet can send water toward a foundation in a downpour even when no river overflows. Check the FEMA flood map for your address at msc.fema.gov.

When containment and a professional are the right call

Call in help when the patch is larger than about 10 square feet, when the wall is wet from floodwater or sewage, when mold keeps returning, or when it sits behind finished walls. The EPA says to use a professional who has experience with buildings damaged by contaminated water.

Larger jobs use negative air containment and HEPA air scrubbers so spores stay in the work area, with remediation following ANSI/IICRC S520. See our mold remediation service for how scope and price are explained before work starts. If the basement is wet, water mitigation usually comes first.
