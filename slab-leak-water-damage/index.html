What a Slab Leak Is, and How Rare It Is Here

Slab leak water damage starts with a leak in a supply, drain or heating line that runs under or inside a concrete floor slab. The water has nowhere to go but up through cracks and joints, or sideways into walls and flooring. It is the kind of leak you often hear or feel before you see.

Here is the honest local picture. Many older Hyattsville houses sit over basements or crawl spaces, so their pipes run through floor joists where leaks tend to show up as stains. That makes slab leaks less common here than in regions where slab-on-grade houses are the norm. No agency we could find publishes a local count, so this page does not quote one.

The call still happens. If you are reading this because your floor is warm, wet or noisy, the sections below cover the real local cases and the first hour. The (301) 936-0619 line runs 24/7, and same-day response is offered when available.

Where Slabs Do Show Up in Hyattsville

Three situations account for the slab leaks that homeowners and renters here are likely to meet. The first is an addition. A sunroom, rear family room or garage conversion added to an older house is often poured on a slab, even when the original house has a basement.

The second is a lower level. Drain lines from a basement bath, laundry or floor drain run under the basement slab, and a separated joint there can leak for months. The third is a ground-floor apartment in a slab-on-grade building. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts puts Hyattsville's owner-occupied rate at 48.4 percent, so about half of households rent, and a renter with a wet floor should tell the landlord in writing the same day.

Age adds risk in every case. The National Register entry says Hyattsville developed mostly after the 1870s, and the city counts nearly 1,000 buildings in its Historic District. Older buildings tend to have older supply lines and drains, and under a slab you cannot inspect them.

First Safety Steps While the Leak Is Active

Water, heat and power can share a slab

Shut the main water valve first. It is often near the water heater or where the supply enters the house. Then look for the cause of any warm spot, because hot water can scald and a heated slab can mean a hot line has failed.

  • If water reaches outlets, appliances or the panel, stay out of the room and call 911 and your electric utility before the line. Cut power only if the panel is dry and safe to reach.
  • If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 and Washington Gas from outside. The leak line is 844-WASHGAS, option 1.
  • If the water smells like sewage or came up through a floor drain, keep people away and call WSSC Water at 301-206-4002.
  • Do not chip or drill into the slab to look for the pipe. Gas, electrical and water lines can share the same floor.

Who Finds the Leak and Who Dries the Floor

A restoration crew does not find or fix the leaking pipe. A licensed plumber does that, often with acoustic listening gear, pressure tests and thermal tools. Some plumbers spot-repair through the slab, some reroute the line through walls or the attic, and drain lines can sometimes be lined in place.

Local rules matter here. WSSC Water's website says it is the inspection agency for plumbing and gas work in Prince George's and Montgomery counties, and that interior re-pipes must be done by a WSSC licensee. Ask any plumber about permits and inspection before work starts.

Responsibility follows the pipe. WSSC says the homeowner maintains the line from the property line to the building, so an underground supply or sewer leak on that stretch is usually yours. Our job starts with the wet building materials, and it runs alongside the plumber's work, not instead of it.

How the Water Damage Gets Dried

  1. 1

    Map the moisture

    Crews use moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera to trace how far water has traveled across the floor and up the walls.

  2. 2

    Remove what traps water

    Wet carpet, pad and some floating floors are lifted so the slab can release moisture. Wet drywall at the base of walls is cut back if it will not dry.

  3. 3

    Dry the slab and the room

    Air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and floor drying mats pull moisture from concrete and air. Concrete holds water longer than wood, so slab drying can take days.

  4. 4

    Recheck after the repair

    Readings are taken daily. The leak must be confirmed fixed, or the slab keeps getting wet and the readings stall.

Does Insurance Cover a Slab Leak?

Sudden versus gradual

The Maryland Insurance Administration says most homeowners policies will not cover damage that results from a slow leak, and many will not pay to repair or replace the pipe or drain itself. A sudden break is treated differently from a leak that went on for months.

Renters policies generally cover your belongings, not the building, per the same agency. Read the water damage and plumbing sections of your policy, photograph everything, and ask your insurer how it treats a leak found under a slab. Never assume a claim will be approved.

Mistakes That Make a Slab Leak Worse

  • ✓Waiting on a warm floor. A hot line leaking under a slab keeps wetting the concrete and the soil underneath.
  • ✓Treating a damp floor as a spill. If the floor stays wet after you dry it, the source is still running.
  • ✓Closing in the floor before readings are dry. New flooring over a wet slab traps moisture, and the water damage repair stage should wait until the slab reads dry.
  • ✓Skipping the drying plan after the plumber leaves. The pipe is fixed, but the slab, wall bases and trim still hold water.
  • ✓Calling the line first when the real danger is gas or power. Call 911 first for either.

The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth, which is hard to meet with concrete, so speed of the first call matters.
