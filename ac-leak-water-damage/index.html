Where AC Water Comes From and Why It Escapes

AC leak water damage is the wet insulation, stained ceilings and soft flooring left behind when an air conditioner's condensate water escapes its drain system. Cooling air pulls moisture out of the air, and that water has to leave through a drain line. When the line clogs or the pan overflows, it goes into the building.

NOAA's 1991 to 2020 normals for the nearest long-term station, Reagan National Airport, put the July mean temperature at 81.0 F. A Mid-Atlantic summer like that keeps cooling systems running for months, so the air handler makes a lot of condensate. A line that is nearly blocked in June may overflow by August.

Because the water drips slowly, the first sign is often a small brown ring on a ceiling. Behind it, insulation may be soaked and framing damp.

AC Leak Safety in the First Hour

Turn it off, then think about the ceiling

Water and wiring share the attic and the air handler closet. Treat any soaked ceiling light or fixture as a hazard.

  • Turn the AC off at the thermostat. Cooling keeps making condensate, and a stopped unit stops the supply of new water.
  • If water is coming through a light fixture or touching outlets, stay out and call 911 and your electric utility. Cut power only if the panel is dry and safe to reach.
  • Do not walk on a sagging ceiling or stand under a bulge. Wet drywall and insulation are heavy and can fall.
  • Call an HVAC company for the unit, and call the line for the water damage. The two jobs are separate.

Condensate Lines, Pans and Float Switches

Most AC leaks come from a short list of parts in the drain path.

Clogged condensate drain line

Algae and debris build up in the line and slow the flow. The water backs up into the pan and then over the edge.

Overflowing drain pan

The pan under the coil catches condensate. A cracked or rusted pan, or one with a blocked outlet, spills onto the surface below.

Failed float switch

Some units have a switch that shuts the system off when the pan fills. If it fails, the unit keeps running while it leaks.

Sweating ducts

The EPA says condensation can form on uninsulated cooling ducts, since warm humid air hitting a cold surface leaves water behind.

What a Drying Visit Involves

  1. 1

    Locate the wet zone

    A moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera show how far water spread across the ceiling, wall cavity and attic floor.

  2. 2

    Remove what will not dry

    Soaked insulation and soft drywall are cut out. Dry sections are kept where possible.

  3. 3

    Dry the structure

    Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run until the framing returns to dry reference points. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard is the published guide, so ask any company if its crews follow it.

  4. 4

    Check for mold and plan repairs

    If mold is present, mold remediation follows ANSI/IICRC S520 containment, HEPA air scrubbers and negative air. Once dry, finishes can be repaired.

Humidity, Mold and the Rest of the House

The EPA's mold guidance says indoor relative humidity should stay below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent. A system that is not draining may leave the home both wet and humid. Mold needs moisture, so the water source has to be fixed before any cleaning makes sense.

The EPA also says to keep air conditioning drip pans clean and the drain lines unobstructed, and to dry water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours. A slow drip that soaks attic insulation for a summer can pass that window many times over, so a wet attic should be checked, not assumed dry. Health questions about mold belong with a physician.

Insurance, Slow Drips and Timing

The Maryland Insurance Administration says most homeowners policies will not cover damage that results from a slow leak. A condensate line that dripped all summer looks like that, while a pan that cracks and overflows in one night may be treated differently. The cost of the HVAC repair itself is usually a separate question. Check your policy or ask your insurer.

The MIA also says mold is excluded in most policies unless it results from a covered peril. Take dated photos and report soon, because Maryland's COMAR 31.15.07.03 sets claim handling timelines for insurers starting at 15 working days to acknowledge a claim, with exceptions.

Mistakes and Prevention

  • ✓Do not keep running the AC after you see a ceiling stain. The unit keeps making water.
  • ✓Do not paint over a stain. Paint hides moisture and does not remove it.
  • ✓Ask an HVAC company to clear and test the condensate line before the cooling season starts.
  • ✓Check the drain pan and the outlet of the drain line a few times each summer if the air handler is accessible.
  • ✓Do not ignore a musty smell in a closet or attic stairwell.
