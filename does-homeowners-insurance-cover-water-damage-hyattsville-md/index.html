Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage in Hyattsville, MD?

The answer depends on how the water got in, how fast, and which endorsements your policy carries. Here is how to sort your own loss before you call anyone.

Short answer: sudden damage is often covered, slow damage often is not

Homeowners insurance often covers water damage that is sudden and accidental, such as a supply line that bursts or a water heater that fails overnight. It usually does not cover damage from a slow leak, long neglect or flooding. Your policy wording decides, so read it or ask your insurer before you assume either way.

The state regulator, the Maryland Insurance Administration, tells consumers that flooding sits outside most homeowners policies, and so does damage that grows out of a slow leak. Mold gets the same treatment: most policies leave it out unless a covered peril caused it. The cause of the water matters as much as the damage you can see.

This guide is general information for Hyattsville, MD, not a coverage opinion. If water is active, call the line for emergency water removal and report the loss to your insurer the same day.

Why the cause of the water matters more than the mess

Insurers sort losses by cause first. A pipe that split on a cold night is a sudden event with a clear start. A trap that wept for months behind a vanity is a gradual one, and the stained cabinet floor and swollen baseboard tell the story.

Adjusters look for signs of age: rust on fasteners, dark rings on framing and layered ceiling stains. Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras map the spread, but they cannot settle coverage. Your insurer makes that call.

Separate the water damage from the failed part. The state insurance regulator cautions that many policies do not pay for repairing or replacing the pipe or drain itself. The wet drywall, flooring and contents may be a different line of the same claim, so ask about each piece.

Flood, sewer backup and the 30-day wait

Flood damage is the loss people most often assume is covered and most often find is not. The regulator's storm guidance says a standard homeowners policy almost never pays for it. Flood cover is its own policy, commonly sold through the National Flood Insurance Program.

As FEMA describes it, an NFIP policy generally takes 30 days to start. That means buying a policy when a storm is in the forecast does not help.

Sewer or water backup is a different animal. The regulator calls it an add-on that insurers are required to offer. If you did not buy it, a backed-up floor drain may fall outside the policy. When contaminated water is involved, the cleanup itself is flood damage restoration work, with extraction, removal of wet materials and drying done under containment where needed.

Claim timelines and what to document

Under COMAR 31.15.07.03, Maryland's rule on unfair claim settlement practices, two kinds of insurer delay count as unfair, with exceptions. One is leaving a claim notice unacknowledged past 15 working days. The other is taking longer than 15 working days to affirm or deny coverage once completed claim forms arrive.

Those rules give you dates to track. Note when you reported the loss, keep copies of every form, and ask for a claim number. Wet materials cannot wait 15 working days, so document first and dry right away.

Record before anything is removed

  • ✓Take wide photos of each wet room, then close-ups of the damage, the source and any visible staining, before furniture moves.
  • ✓Photograph the failed pipe, hose or fixture, and keep the part if you can.
  • ✓List damaged belongings with a brief description, and keep receipts or model numbers if you have them.
  • ✓Write down the date and time you found the water, when you shut it off and who you called.
  • ✓Save moisture readings and any drying logs a restoration crew gives you.

How a written scope helps when you talk to your insurer

A written scope lists the rooms affected, the water category, the materials to be removed or dried, and the equipment planned, including air movers and LGR dehumidifiers. This business walks you through scope and price ahead of any work, so you see that document before agreeing to anything. You can hand a copy to your insurer, who decides what the policy pays.

Photos, drying readings and the written plan that match the cause you reported leave less to guess at. Ask whether the crew follows ANSI/IICRC S500, the published water damage restoration standard.
