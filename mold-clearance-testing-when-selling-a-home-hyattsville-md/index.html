Mold Clearance Testing Before Closing: A Hyattsville, MD Seller's Guide

When a sale is waiting on mold paperwork, the vocabulary gets confusing fast. Here is what the terms mean and what to ask.

What a clearance request actually asks for

Sellers often hear this late in the deal. The buyer's lender or home inspector flags mold and says the sale cannot move ahead until it is cleaned and shown to be cleaned. The request usually means two things: the mold must be remediated, and someone should confirm the result.

That confirmation is called post-remediation verification or clearance testing. It is a check after the work, usually a visual inspection plus sampling, to show the area meets the cleanup goals. ANSI/IICRC S520 is the published procedural standard for remediation of mold-damaged structures and contents.

No company can promise a particular lender, buyer or inspector will accept a result. What you can do is make the paper trail clear, with the cause fixed, the work documented and the testing done independently.

Four terms you will hear on the way to closing

Remediation

The cleanup work: containing the area, removing affected porous materials, cleaning surfaces and drying. Negative air containment and HEPA air scrubbers are common tools.

Post-remediation verification

A check after the work that the area is visibly clean and dry. Sometimes the remediation company does it, and sometimes someone else does.

Independent clearance testing

Testing done by a party that is not the company doing the cleanup. This separation is the reason it carries weight with many buyers and lenders.

S520

The standard that sets out how mold remediation work is planned and carried out. You can ask any company whether its crews follow it.

Why testing from a separate party is the common ask

If the same company cleans the mold and then says it is clear, the buyer's side has only the seller's contractor's word. A separate tester has no stake in the cleanup, so the finding is easier for others to trust. That is why requests often name an independent party.

Ask whoever is making the request what they want to see. Some want a written report, some want lab results, some want both. Get the requirement in writing before the work starts, so you do not pay for the wrong thing.

Mold remediation done to S520 can include a written scope, containment and documentation that a tester can build on. The testing itself is a separate step handled by someone else.

Fix the water first, or the paperwork will not hold

Mold needs moisture, so the source comes first

The EPA says mold will not grow without water or moisture, so cleaning a wall while a leak keeps feeding it solves nothing. A failed clearance usually traces back to moisture that was never fixed.

Typical sources include a toilet or sink leak, an AC condensate line, a roof or siding leak, or basement seepage. Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras help confirm the area is dry before and after the work. Water mitigation addresses the drying side when the cause was a water loss.

Hyattsville, MD: licensing and insurance facts to know

The Maryland Home Improvement Commission FAQ says that a 2008 law to license mold remediators terminated on July 1, 2019. Maryland does not currently run a mold remediation license under that 2008 law, so ask any company what training and standards it follows.

The Maryland Insurance Administration says mold is excluded in most policies unless it results from a covered peril, and that most homeowners policies will not cover damage that results from a slow leak. Check your policy and ask your insurer before you assume the cleanup is covered.

The city's resident brochure says most construction work requires a permit from Prince George's DPIE. Permit rules vary by the work, so ask DPIE or the city before rebuilding walls, electrical or plumbing after the mold is removed.

Questions for an attorney or agent, not a contractor

Whether and how you must tell a buyer about mold is a question for a Maryland real estate attorney or your agent. A restoration company cannot answer it, and neither can a web article. The same goes for contract deadlines and what happens if a lender will not fund.

What we can say is practical. Keep invoices, scopes, moisture logs and test reports in one folder, because every party will ask for them.

Questions to ask any remediation company

A seller on a deadline should compare companies on process, not just speed.

  • ✓Does your crew work to ANSI/IICRC S520, and will the written scope say so?
  • ✓What training does your crew have, given that Maryland does not currently run a mold remediation license under the 2008 law?
  • ✓How will you contain the area, and will you use negative air containment and HEPA air scrubbers?
  • ✓Will the scope and price be explained in writing before work starts?
  • ✓What documents will I have at the end that my agent can share with the buyer's side?
  • ✓How do you handle the moisture source, and who confirms the area is dry?

What a clean result does and does not show

A passing clearance result describes the area on the day it was tested. It shows the cleanup goals were met then. It does not predict the future, because mold needs moisture, and any new leak can start the problem again.

That is why sellers keep the moisture records alongside the test report. Readings from a moisture meter after the repair show the building was dry when the work ended, and a buyer's inspector can compare them with their own.

If a buyer asks for more testing, ask who will pay for it and which party will choose the tester. Settle that in writing, with your agent, before anyone books a visit.
